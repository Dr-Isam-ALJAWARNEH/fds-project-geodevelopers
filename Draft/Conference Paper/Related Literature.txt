This study [2] presents a thorough examination of the relationships between particulate matter (PM2.5), nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and ground-level ozone (O3) in terms of their concentrations in three cities influenced by meteorological conditions. The correlation between PM2.5 and NO2 is discovered to be negative between temperature and both NO2 and PM concentrations including PM2.5 which suggests that higher temperatures are associated with lower concentrations of NO2 and PM2.5. It is observed that O3 concentrations are positively correlated with temperature with correlation values of 0.51, 0.74, and 0.46 in Krakow, Milan, and Paris, respectively reflecting that as temperatures rise, O3 levels also increase. Moreover, PM2.5 tends to have higher concentrations during cooler months and lower temperatures in hotter months. Additionally, O3 concentrations were found to be higher on weekends due to decreased anthropogenic emissions, which can also affect the levels of NO2 and PM2.5. It is necessary to understand these relationships for developing air quality management strategies.
The Dijkstra algorithm is the best approach to analyzing the shortest path, with the existing work that improves this algorithm. This research study mainly focuses on  how to get the best path for optimization using the A* algorithm under a mobile gas environment [3].  The Dijkstra algorithm is used to find out the shortest paths through a road network. A* algorithm is a learning technique used to optimize routing decisions based on various factors [4]. A* algorithm was first proposed by Peter Hart, Nils Nilsson, and Raphael. Unlike the Dijkstra algorithm, which doesn’t know how far the final state, the A* algorithm has a vertex from start to current and from current to the desired state. While it is not a breadth-first search, the A* algorithm is a heuristic search method commonly used to find the shortest path [3]. To get the best path from source to destination we used a specific  function of A* algorithm which is : 
f(j) = g(j) + h(j)
Where g (n) is the path cost from the starting state to vertex n, and h (n) is the approximate cost of the shortest path from vertex (n) to the desired state, so we have f (n) = the approximate cost of the best path that continues from vertex (n) to the desired goal. [4]
